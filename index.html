The Case for Nuclear at Shoreham

We live in a power trap. Nuclear is the way out.

Long Island is NYISO Zone K — an islanded grid with limited transmission into it. Even when power is cheap elsewhere in New York, it can't reach us. Roughly 90% of our on-island generation burns natural gas, so every fuel spike lands directly on your bill.

Affordability

Break the gas dependency

The Power Supply Charge on your bill moves with natural gas markets — it jumped over 13% in a single month in early 2026. On-island nuclear baseload means stable fuel costs for 60–80 years and less exposure to price shocks Long Island can't control.

Reliability

Firm power for an island grid

Nuclear plants run at 90%+ capacity factors — day, night, heat wave, nor'easter. As Long Island electrifies homes, cars, and businesses, Zone K needs firm zero-emission generation inside the load pocket, not just imports that can't physically reach us.

Economy

Jobs and a stronger tax base

A modern nuclear facility means hundreds of permanent, high-wage union careers, thousands of construction jobs, and decades of tax revenue for the Shoreham-Wading River schools and Brookhaven — with Brookhaven National Laboratory's nuclear science expertise right down the road.

The History

The plant that never was — and the moment that's here now.

Shoreham is the only fully completed U.S. nuclear plant decommissioned without ever entering commercial operation. Its story explains our bills today — and why the second chance matters.

1965–1973

LILCO proposes Shoreham

The Long Island Lighting Company announces a nuclear plant on Long Island Sound, originally estimated under $100 million. Construction of the 820 MW GE boiling water reactor begins in 1973.

1979

Three Mile Island changes everything

The partial meltdown in Pennsylvania supercharges opposition. Some 15,000 people rally at Shoreham. Evacuation planning for Long Island's geography becomes the central fight.

1984–1989

Complete — but never switched on

The finished plant receives a low-power testing license and runs at 5% capacity. That's as far as it ever gets. Suffolk County refuses to approve evacuation plans; costs balloon to $5.5–6 billion. In 1989, the state brokers a deal to shut it down.

1992–1994

Sold for one dollar, dismantled

LILCO sells Shoreham to the Long Island Power Authority for $1. Fuel and reactor internals are removed and the plant is decommissioned — the first in U.S. history to be retired without ever going commercial. Ratepayers absorb the debt, which won't be paid off until 2033.

1998–2025

The long hangover

LIPA replaces LILCO. Long Island's rates stay among the nation's highest. The site hosts a small gas peaker, two wind turbines, and — approved in 2026 — battery storage. The grid connection, substation, and industrial site remain.

2025–2026

New York goes nuclear again

Governor Hochul directs NYPA to build at least 1 GW of new advanced nuclear and announces an 8.4 GW statewide Nuclear Reliability Backbone. Plants like Palisades and Three Mile Island are being restarted nationwide. Public opinion has shifted. The question is no longer whether New York builds nuclear — it's where.

NEXT

Shoreham's second chance

The original reactor is gone — this is not about flipping an old switch. It's about siting a new generation of advanced reactors on a site that already has grid interconnection, coastal cooling water, industrial zoning, and a community that has paid nuclear prices for 40 years without nuclear power. Long Island deserves a seat at the table in New York's nuclear buildout.

Straight Answers

Questions Long Islanders are asking

Can you just restart the old Shoreham reactor?

No — and we won't pretend otherwise. The original reactor's fuel and internals were removed during decommissioning in the early 1990s. Unlike Palisades or Three Mile Island, there's nothing to restart. What Shoreham has is arguably more valuable for new construction: an existing grid interconnection and substation, coastal cooling water, industrial zoning, and transmission infrastructure. This campaign is about siting new advanced reactors — including small modular reactors (SMRs) — at or near the site.

What about the evacuation concerns that killed Shoreham the first time?

Modern advanced reactor designs are fundamentally different from 1970s designs — passive safety systems that shut down without power or operator action, smaller cores, and lower operating pressures. The NRC's emergency planning frameworks for SMRs reflect these smaller risk profiles, with some designs qualifying for emergency planning zones limited to the site boundary itself rather than a 10-mile radius. Those are exactly the questions a formal siting study should answer publicly and rigorously.

Would this actually lower my electric bill?

No single plant fixes everything, but the fundamentals point the right way: Long Island pays a premium because we're an import-constrained island burning natural gas. Firm on-island generation with stable fuel costs reduces both the Zone K congestion premium and our exposure to gas price spikes. First-of-a-kind nuclear is expensive to build — which is why state and federal financing support, and New York's fleet-style deployment approach, matter so much.

Why not just build more solar, wind, and batteries?

We should — and Long Island is. But renewables and storage alone can't cover multi-day winter demand on an islanded grid, and offshore wind projects have faced their own delays and cost overruns. New York's own energy plan treats nuclear as the firm, zero-emission complement to renewables, not a competitor to them. The Shoreham site already hosts wind turbines and approved battery storage; nuclear would complete the picture.

Who is behind this campaign?

Shoreham Power is an independent citizen advocacy effort. We are not affiliated with LIPA, PSEG Long Island, NYPA, or any reactor vendor. Our goal is simple: get Long Island formally included in New York's nuclear siting conversation, starting with a state-commissioned feasibility study for the Shoreham site.
